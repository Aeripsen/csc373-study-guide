The whole course, built around the exam paper

Seven units, seven exam questions, and the exact answer skeleton each one wants.

This course looks like six unrelated topics. It is not. It is seven question types, and the exam asks one of each, in the same order, every year. Every page here is organised around that fact: what the question looks like, why the technique works, and the precise thing you write on the paper.

What the exam actually is

The 40% rule

"In order to pass the course, you must achieve a grade of at least 40% on this final examination." That is 36 of 90 marks. You cannot bank enough term work to skip this. The safest 36 marks on the paper are Q4 (flow, mechanical) and Q6 (LP, mechanical), plus the first part of Q2 and the certificate half of Q7. Learn those cold before you touch anything hard.

Where the final sits in the course mark

Quizzes 3%, four homework assignments 24%, the embedded ethics module 5%, term test 1 20%, term test 2 10%, and the final exam 38%. Term test 1 covers divide and conquer, dynamic programming and greedy. Term test 2 covers network flow, linear programming and complexity. The final is comprehensive, which is why this site is organised by question type rather than by week.

Read the mark split before you read the question

Q4 and Q6 are 20 marks of pure procedure with nothing to invent. Q2 hands you 2 marks just for saying what the array holds. Q7 hands you roughly 3 for naming a certificate and a certifier. That is about 25 marks available to anyone who has memorised a format, before a single creative idea. The creative marks live in Q1, Q3, Q5 and the reduction half of Q7.

Three things that decide your grade

1. The question tells you the technique

Every exam question is titled with its unit. Q1 says "Divide and Conquer" above it. You are never asked to work out which paradigm applies, which means the hard part of algorithm design is deleted for you. Your job is to fit the problem to a shape you already know.

2. The running time is stated in the question

"For full credit, your algorithm should run in time O(n)". That is not a constraint, it is a hint. O(n log n) means sort first, or divide and conquer with linear merging. O(nB) means a DP table with n rows and B columns. O(n) means one pass, so greedy. Read the bound and it tells you the answer shape before you have thought about the problem.

3. Marks come from structure, not insight

Compare the model solutions and they all have the same skeleton. Q2 gives 2 marks for one sentence defining the array. Q3 gives half the marks for a proof whose first line is always "we prove by induction that after step i, the greedy partial solution is contained in some optimal solution". You can write that first line before you have solved the problem.

On notation

Everything here uses the notation from the course itself: c[j] style arrays, fin(v) and fout(v) for flow, X ≤P Y for polynomial time reduction, and the master theorem stated with c = logb(a). If a symbol here looks unfamiliar, it is a typo on this site, not a new convention.
