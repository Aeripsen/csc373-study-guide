What goes on your aid sheet

Space is the constraint. Anything you would derive in under a minute is a waste of it.

The principle

An aid sheet is not a summary of the course. It is a list of things you would otherwise get wrong or slow under pressure. Two categories earn space: exact statements you cannot risk misremembering (the master theorem, the dual rule), and answer skeletons that save you from writing yourself into a corner.

Concepts you understand do not go on it. If you understand why the backward edge exists, writing "backward edge = f(u,v)" costs a line and buys nothing. Write the things that are arbitrary, not the things that are reasoned.

Read this before you make the sheet

On the final it is not the instructor who checks your aid sheet, it is an invigilator sent by the Faculty of Arts and Science, and they are strict. If it is not handwritten on a piece of paper, they will make you put it away or confiscate it, and write you up. Screen grabs of PDFs pasted onto a sheet, printed text, or anything that does not look like something you could have written by hand will be treated as an unauthorised aid.

Students have been badly rattled by this at the start of an exam. Do not take the risk for the sake of a neater sheet. Write it by hand, on paper, in pen.

Side 1: the exact statements

Master theorem, written out in full

Copy this verbatim

T(n) ≤ aT(⌈n/b⌉) + f(n),   a ≥ 1, b > 1,   c = logb(a).

  • f(n) = O(nc-ε), some ε > 0  ⇒  T(n) = O(nc)
  • f(n) = Θ(nc log(n)k), k ≥ 0  ⇒  T(n) = O(nc log(n)k+1)
  • f(n) = Ω(nc+ε), some ε > 0, and af(n/b) ≤ df(n) for a constant d < 1  ⇒  T(n) = O(f(n))

Plus the four worked values: log₂2 = 1, log₂3 ≈ 1.585, log₂8 = 3, log₄2 = 0.5.

It is very likely given to you

The instructor has said outright that a statement of the master theorem is provided on the exam sheet, and it was printed at the top of both past midterms. So this is insurance rather than a necessity. It costs six lines, and if it turns out to be printed on the paper you have lost almost nothing. The four log values are worth keeping regardless, since those are not provided.

Complexity definitions, exactly worded

Copy this verbatim 
X ∈ NP:  poly-time B and poly p with
           x ∈ X  ⇔  ∃y, |y| ≤ p(|x|), B(x,y) = Yes

X ≤ᵧ Y:   poly-time R with  x ∈ X  ⇔  R(x) ∈ Y

NP-hard:      Y ≤ᵧ X  for every Y ∈ NP
NP-complete:  in NP  AND  NP-hard

TO PROVE Y NP-COMPLETE:
1. certificate + certifier            (Y ∈ NP)
2. pick known NPC X, give R           (X ≤ᵧ Y, X is the SOURCE)
3. Yes for X  ⇒  Yes for R(X)
4. Yes for R(X)  ⇒  Yes for X
5. "in NP and NP-hard, therefore NP-complete"

ARROW POINTS FROM THE KNOWN HARD PROBLEM INTO YOURS

known NPC you may reduce FROM (confirmed by course staff):
  3-SAT, Subset-Sum, Independent-Set, Vertex-Cover,
  Set-Cover, 3-Colouring, BILP
  stuck? 3-SAT works in most cases

S independent in G  ⇔  V\S is a vertex cover  ⇔  S is a clique in Ḡ

Side 2: the answer skeletons

These are the ones that stop you writing yourself into a corner at minute 90. Write them as compressed prompts, not full sentences, since you only need the cue.

Greedy proof skeleton 
Claim: after step i, Sᵢ ⊆ some optimal O
base: S₀ = ∅
step: (1) skipped i  →  done
      (2) took i, i ∈ O  →  done
      (3) took i, i ∉ O  →  e = conflicting elt of O,
          O' = O - e + i
          feasible BECAUSE [greedy rule]   ← the only real content
          |O'| = |O| so optimal;  Sᵢ ⊆ O'
end:  S maximal + O feasible + S ⊆ O  ⇒  S = O
DP skeleton 
(a) For [range], A[i] = [max/min/count of] ... . Answer = A[n].
(b) recurrence + BASE CASES labelled
    justify: "optimal soln either does X (→ A[..] + v)
              or not (→ A[..]); exhaustive; take the better"
(c) fill order (all deps have smaller index)
    pseudocode, nested loops
    "nB entries, O(1) each  ⇒  O(nB)"
(d) IF OUTPUT ASKED: second array J[i] = argmax,
    walk backwards from n
Flow modelling skeleton 
V' = s, t, uᵢ per left thing, vⲧ per right thing
(s,uᵢ) cap = per-left limit
(uᵢ,vⲧ) cap = 1,  ONLY for permitted pairs
(vⲧ,t) cap = per-right limit
global cap c?  →  extra node t', edge (t',t) cap c

algorithm: integral max flow by Edmonds-Karp; assign when f = 1
correctness BOTH WAYS:
  assignment size k  →  flow value k  (capacities hold because ...)
  integral flow k  →  assignment k  (INTEGRALITY + conservation)
time: |V'|, |E'| counted, then O(|E'|²|V'|)
Flow mechanics 
residual: forward c-f,  backward f       BOTH, ALWAYS
min cut:  A = reachable from s in G_f
2nd cut:  B = can reach t in G_f
c(A,B) = sum of caps A→B only, B→A contributes 0
check:   c(A,B) must equal val(f)

FF: O(|E|·C), pseudo-poly
Edmonds-Karp (shortest path): O(|E|²|V|), polynomial
bipartite matching: O(mn)
D&C skeleton 
"Procedure Solve(A,l,u) returns [exact contract]"
  ← if combine is too slow, make the recursion ALSO return
    something (usually sorted order)
pseudocode: base, split, 2 recursive calls, combine
correctness: induction on n
time: T(n) ≤ aT(n/b) + f(n), master theorem sentence

What not to write

These waste space
  • Full pseudocode for algorithms from class. You are never asked to reproduce mergesort or Ford Fulkerson verbatim, only to apply or adapt them. A one line reminder of the idea is enough.
  • Worked examples. They eat enormous space and the exam's numbers will differ anyway.
  • Definitions you understand. Flow conservation, what a matching is, what a cut is. If you could explain it to someone, you do not need it written down.
  • Big-O rules and log identities. If you need log(ab) = log a + log b written down, the aid sheet is not your problem.
  • The proof of max flow min cut. You will not be asked to reproduce it. The three equivalent statements are worth a line, the proof is not.
A test for every line

Ask: "if this were not on the sheet, would I get it wrong or would I just be slower?" Write down the things you would get wrong. Being slower is survivable, being wrong is not, and the arbitrary conventions (which way the dual flips, which reduction direction, whether case 3 has a side condition) are exactly the things that go wrong at minute 100 of a three hour exam.

Write it by hand, twice

The rule says handwritten, which is an accident that works in your favour. Writing it out is itself a revision pass, and writing it out twice, a first draft and then a tighter final, forces you to decide what actually matters. Most people find the second version is half the length and twice as useful.

Do the final version at least two days before the exam, not the night before, so you have time to practise with the sheet you will actually take in. A sheet you have never used under time pressure is a sheet you will not be able to find anything on.
