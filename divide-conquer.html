The shape of every D&C question

Three steps, and only one of them is ever interesting.

  1. Divide the input into similar subproblems, usually two halves.
  2. Recurse to solve them.
  3. Combine the sub-answers into the answer for the whole.
Where the thinking goes

Divide is always "cut it in half". Recurse is always "call yourself". Neither earns marks. The entire question is the combine step: what do you have to compute that neither half knows about?

For counting inversions, the halves know their own inversions, so combine has to count the pairs that straddle the split. For closest pair, the halves know their own closest pairs, so combine has to find any closer pair with one point on each side. Once you name the straddling quantity, you have the algorithm.

The move that makes combining fast

The linear time combine almost always comes from demanding more from the recursion than the problem asks for. Counting inversions is fast because each recursive call also returns its half sorted. That extra promise costs nothing (the merge does it anyway) and turns an O(n2) combine into an O(n) one.

On the exam, if your combine step is too slow, ask what extra thing the recursion could return that would speed it up. That is nearly always the intended trick.

Counting inversions

Input: an array A[1..n] of distinct integers.
Output: the number of pairs i < j with A[i] > A[j].

The brute force is to check all pairs, O(n2). It measures how far from sorted A is, which is also the Kendall tau distance between two rankings, used in voting theory and rank aggregation.

Split A into B (left half) and C (right half). Every inversion is one of three kinds: both indices in B, both in C, or one in each. The first two are what the recursive calls return. The third is what combine has to count.

Merge and count

B and C arrive sorted. Every time we take an element from C, it is smaller than everything still left in B, so it forms an inversion with each of them. Add the count of what remains in B.

Two sorted halves. Compare the front of each. Press Step.
Why taking from C is the counting moment

B and C are both sorted, and everything in B started to the left of everything in C. So if the front of C is smaller than the front of B, it is smaller than every remaining element of B, and each of those is a pair i < j with A[i] > A[j]. That is |B remaining| inversions discovered in one comparison, which is how a whole quadratic count collapses into a linear pass.

Taking from B tells you nothing new, because a B element being smaller is the non inverted case.

The algorithm

Sort-and-Count(A)
1  if A has length 1
2      return (0, A)
3  B = A[1 .. n/2]
4  C = A[n/2 + 1 .. n]
5  (inv_B, B) = Sort-and-Count(B)
6  (inv_C, C) = Sort-and-Count(C)
7  (inv_BC, A) = Merge-and-Count(B, C)
8  return (inv_B + inv_C + inv_BC, A)

Line 7 is the merge from mergesort with a counter bolted on. Note the contract: the procedure returns a pair, the count and the sorted array. Lines 5 and 6 rely on that.

Running time. T(n) = 2T(n/2) + O(n), and the master theorem gives O(n log n).

The variant they actually ask

The October 2025 midterm asked for significant inversions: pairs with A[i] > 2A[j]. The fix is small and worth memorising as a pattern. Make a copy of the sorted right half, double every entry, and run the counting merge against that copy. Then do a second, plain merge of the two original halves to produce the sorted output.

Two passes, each O(n), so the recurrence is unchanged. The reason you need two is that the doubled array is the right thing for counting and the wrong thing for sorting. Whenever the comparison used for counting differs from the comparison used for ordering, you need separate passes.

The master theorem

This is the tool for turning a recurrence into a bound, and it is stated on the exam paper in this form. It was printed on both past midterms as a reference. Put it on your aid sheet anyway.

Master theorem, as stated in this course

Let a ≥ 1, b > 1 be constants, and T(n) ≤ aT(⌈n/b⌉) + f(n). Let c = logb(a). Then:

  • Case 1 (leaves win). If f(n) = O(nc-ε) for some ε > 0, then T(n) = O(nc).
  • Case 2 (tie). If f(n) = Θ(nc log(n)k) for k ≥ 0, then T(n) = O(nc log(n)k+1).
  • Case 3 (root wins). If f(n) = Ω(nc+ε) for some ε > 0, and af(n/b) ≤ df(n) for some constant d < 1 and all large n, then T(n) = O(f(n)).

Why the three cases exist

Each bar is the total work done at one level of the recursion tree. Change a and b and watch which level dominates. That is the whole content of the theorem.

The intuition in one line

At depth i there are ai subproblems, each of size n/bi, so the work at that level is ai f(n/bi). That is a geometric series in disguise. If it grows going down, the bottom level dominates and the answer is the number of leaves, nc. If it shrinks, the top dominates and the answer is f(n). If it is flat, every one of the logbn levels costs the same, so you multiply by a log.

You never need to remember which case is which. Compare nc with f(n), and the bigger one wins. A tie costs you a log factor.

Applying it in the exam, in 20 seconds

  1. Read off a (number of recursive calls) and b (shrink factor).
  2. Compute c = logba.
  3. Compare f(n) to nc. Bigger one wins, tie gives an extra log.
  4. Write the sentence: "With a = _, b = _, c = logba = _, and f(n) = _, we are in case _, so T(n) = _."

Case 1 needs a gap, not just "smaller"

Row 2 above catches people out. Is n log n smaller than n1.58? Yes, and by a polynomial factor: n log n = O(n1.58-ε) holds for small ε, because log n grows slower than any nε. So case 1 applies and the log disappears entirely from the answer.

The published solution spells this out: "because n log n = O(n1+ε) for any ε > 0, and log2(3) > 1". Write that parenthetical. It is the justification the question is asking for.

Case 3 has a second condition and people forget it

af(n/b) ≤ df(n) for some d < 1. It is a regularity condition saying f really does shrink as you go down. For every polynomial f it holds trivially, so on the exam you check it in one line and move on: for T(n) = 2T(n/4) + O(n), af(n/b) = 2(n/4) = n/2 = ½f(n), so d = ½ works.

Closest pair of points

Input: n points in the plane. Output: the two points at minimum Euclidean distance. Brute force is O(n2). In one dimension you would just sort and check adjacent pairs, but in two dimensions there is no single order that puts close points next to each other, so that fails.

Divide with a vertical line L into equal halves Q and R. Recurse to get the closest pair in each. Let δ be the smaller of the two distances. Combine: is there a pair closer than δ with one point in Q and one in R?

The strip, and why 11 comparisons suffice

Only points within δ of the line can be part of a closer straddling pair. Sort that strip by y, and each point needs to be compared with only the next 11.

Points in the plane. Press Step to divide.
Where the 11 comes from

Cut the strip into squares of side δ/2. Each square holds at most one point: it lies entirely on one side of L, and its diagonal is δ√2/2 ≈ 0.71δ < δ, so two points inside it would be closer than δ, contradicting what the recursion already found.

The strip is 2δ wide, so each row holds 4 squares. If two points in the y sorted order are more than 11 positions apart, they are separated by at least 3 full rows, so their vertical distance exceeds δ and they cannot be the pair you are looking for. Comparing each point to the next 11 is therefore enough, and 11 is a constant, so the scan is O(n).

Running time. Naively T(n) = 2T(n/2) + O(n log n) because the strip has to be sorted by y, giving O(n log2 n). Sort by both coordinates once at the start and reuse those orders, and the combine drops to O(n), giving T(n) = 2T(n/2) + O(n) and O(n log n).

Karatsuba, or 4 multiplications becoming 3

Input: two n bit integers x and y. Output: their product. Grade school long multiplication is O(n2), and Kolmogorov conjectured that was optimal. Karatsuba disproved it.

Split each number in half: x = x₁2n/2 + x₀ and y = y₁2n/2 + y₀. Then

xy = x₁y₁·2ⁿ + (x₁y₀ + x₀y₁)·2^(n/2) + x₀y₀

Computing all four products separately gives T(n) = 4T(n/2) + O(n), and since c = log₂4 = 2, that is O(n2). No gain.

The trick

You do not need x₁y₀ and x₀y₁ separately, only their sum. And

(x₁ + x₀)(y₁ + y₀) = x₁y₁ + (x₁y₀ + x₀y₁) + x₀y₀

so the middle term is that product minus the two you already computed. Three recursive multiplications instead of four:

  • A = x₁y₁
  • B = x₀y₀
  • C = (x₁ + x₀)(y₁ + y₀), and the middle term is C - A - B

T(n) = 3T(n/2) + O(n), and c = log₂3 ≈ 1.585 with f(n) = n smaller, so case 1 gives O(n1.585).

Worth knowing, not worth memorising

Schonhage and Strassen got O(n log n log log n) in 1971 using the fast Fourier transform, and Harvey and van der Hoeven reached O(n log n) in 2021. This is background, not examinable procedure.

Exam answer, written out

The December 2024 Q1, done the way you would actually write it. The problem: n jobs, each with a salary si and a fun score fi, all distinct. Job i dominates job j if fi > fj and si > sj. A job is good if nothing dominates it. Output all good jobs in O(n log n).

Think about it first, then open this

Algorithm. Pre-process by sorting the jobs by salary in O(n log n). Then run Good(l, u), which returns the list of jobs in l..u that are not dominated by any other job in l..u, sorted by salary.

Good(l, u)
1  if l == u
2      return [job l]
3  m = (l + u) / 2
4  L = Good(l, m)          // lower salaries
5  R = Good(m + 1, u)      // higher salaries
6  fmax = max fun score among jobs m+1 .. u
7  L' = [ job in L with fun score > fmax ]
8  return L' followed by R

In words. Split by salary at the median. Nothing in the low half can dominate anything in the high half, because domination needs a strictly higher salary. So every good job of the high half is still good overall, and R passes through untouched. A job in the low half survives only if no high half job dominates it. Every high half job already beats it on salary, so it survives exactly when its fun score beats all of them, that is, when it beats the maximum fun score in the high half. One number, computed in one linear pass, filters the whole left list.

Correctness. By induction on u - l. The base case of one job is immediate, since a single job is dominated by nothing. For the step, assume L and R are correct for their halves. A job in the high half dominated by nothing in its own half is dominated by nothing at all, since every low half job has a smaller salary, so returning R unchanged is right. A job x in the low half is dominated by some job in the whole range exactly when it is dominated within its own half (excluded already, by the induction hypothesis) or by some high half job y. Since sy > sx automatically, that happens exactly when some high half job has a larger fun score, that is, when fx < fmax. Line 7 removes exactly those.

Running time. Sorting once costs O(n log n). Lines 6 and 7 are single passes over at most n elements, so the combine is O(n) and T(n) = 2T(n/2) + O(n). By the master theorem with a = 2, b = 2, c = log22 = 1 and f(n) = Θ(n), we are in case 2 with k = 0, so T(n) = O(n log n). Adding the sort leaves the bound at O(n log n).

The move to notice

The combine step compressed the entire right half into one number, its maximum fun score. That is the recurring pattern in D&C combine steps: find the single summary statistic of one half that is enough to filter the other. Sorted order, a maximum, a count, a boundary strip. If your combine is looping over both halves at once, you have missed the summary.

Recognising Q1 in 60 seconds
  • Target is O(n log n) and the input is an array or a point set. Split in half.
  • The question hints at a sort or a median as a pre-processing step. That is the divide rule handed to you.
  • Ask: what is true of a pair that straddles the split, and can I summarise one side into something small enough to check it in one pass?
  • If the combine seems to need O(n log n), ask what the recursion could also return (usually sorted order) to make it O(n).
