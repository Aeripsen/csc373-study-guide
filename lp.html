What an LP is, and the geometry

A linear program optimises a linear objective subject to linear inequalities. Every constraint is a half plane, their intersection is a convex polyhedron (the feasible region), and you slide the objective as far as it will go inside it.

Why the optimum sits at a corner

Drag the objective direction. The dashed line is a level set of the objective, pushed as far as it goes. It stops at a vertex, and only ties at an edge.

The consequence that matters

An optimum is always attained at a vertex of the polyhedron (whenever one exists and is finite). There are finitely many vertices, so the problem is finite, which is why algorithms exist at all.

Simplex walks from vertex to neighbouring vertex, improving each time. It is fast in practice and exponential in the worst case. Interior point methods cut through the middle and run in time polynomial in the number of bits of the input, about O((m+n)3.5L) for m constraints, n variables, and L bit coefficients. You need the fact that LP is solvable in polynomial time, not the algorithms themselves.

Standard form, and the four moves

The two standard forms
MaximisationMinimisation
 
max  c₁x₁ + ... + cₙxₙ
s.t. a₁₁x₁ + ... + a₁ₙxₙ ≤ b₁
     ...
     aₘ₁x₁ + ... + aₘₙxₙ ≤ bₘ
     x₁, ..., xₙ ≥ 0
 
min  c₁x₁ + ... + cₙxₙ
s.t. a₁₁x₁ + ... + a₁ₙxₙ ≥ b₁
     ...
     aₘ₁x₁ + ... + aₘₙxₙ ≥ bₘ
     x₁, ..., xₙ ≥ 0

Max goes with ≤, min goes with ≥, and every variable is non negative in both. Remembering that pairing is most of the battle.

The four moves, in this order
  1. Collect variables on the left, constants on the right. x₂ ≤ 1 - x₁ becomes x₁ + x₂ ≤ 1. Do this first, it prevents most sign errors.
  2. Flip the wrong direction inequalities by multiplying by -1, which reverses the sign. 2x₂ ≥ x₁ becomes x₁ - 2x₂ ≤ 0.
  3. Split equalities. g(x) = b becomes g(x) ≤ b and g(x) ≥ b, then flip the second.
  4. Split free variables. If xi may be negative, replace every occurrence with xi+ - xi- and add xi+, xi- ≥ 0. In the objective too.

To turn a min into a max, negate the objective: min f and max -f have the same optimiser, and the optimal values differ by a sign. Say which convention you are using.

Why splitting a free variable works

Every real number is a difference of two non negative reals, and the map is onto: given any x, take x+ = max(x, 0) and x- = max(-x, 0). So the feasible values of x+ - x- are exactly all of ℝ, and the LP is unchanged. The representation is not unique (you could add 5 to both), but nothing in the LP can tell the difference, so it does not matter.

The dual, and where it comes from

The mechanical rule
Primal (max)Dual (min)
max cTxmin bTy
Ax ≤ bATy ≥ c
x ≥ 0y ≥ 0
m constraintsm variables
n variablesn constraints

One dual variable per primal constraint. One dual constraint per primal variable. The old right hand side becomes the new objective, the old objective becomes the new right hand side, and the matrix is transposed.

Then the sentence the exam asks for every time: "the dual variable yi corresponds to the primal constraint [name it]."

Where the dual actually comes from

It is not an arbitrary transformation. It is the search for the best provable upper bound on the primal.

Suppose you want to bound max cTx. Take non negative multipliers y₁, ..., ym, multiply constraint i by yi, and add them all up. Since every yi ≥ 0, the inequalities survive, and you get

(∑ᵢ yᵢaᵢ₁)x₁ + ... + (∑ᵢ yᵢaᵢₙ)xₙ  ≤  ∑ᵢ yᵢbᵢ

If the coefficient of each xj on the left is at least cj, then because x ≥ 0, the left side is at least the objective. So cTx ≤ bTy: you have proved an upper bound.

The requirement "coefficient of xj is at least cj" is exactly ATy ≥ c. Minimising bTy is asking for the tightest such bound. That is the dual, and weak duality is true by construction.

The duality theorem

Statement

For a primal and dual pair, exactly one of the following holds:

  1. Both are feasible, and their optimal values are equal and finite.
  2. One is unbounded, and the other is infeasible.
  3. Both are infeasible.

Vocabulary: an LP is feasible if some point satisfies all its constraints, and unbounded if its objective can be pushed to infinity (or minus infinity for a min).

The connection to max flow min cut

Max flow min cut is a special case of this theorem. Write max flow as an LP: variables are the f(e), constraints are capacity and conservation. Take its dual and you get, after a little work, the minimum cut LP. Strong duality then says the two optimal values are equal, which is the max flow min cut theorem.

The pattern is the same one you saw in flow: a cut certifies that a flow is maximum, and a dual solution certifies that a primal solution is optimal. If you can hand someone a feasible primal and a feasible dual with the same objective value, both are optimal and no further argument is needed. Assignment 4 used exactly this to bound the minimum flow by a forward cut.

Modelling: absolute values and min max

An LP objective must be linear, so |x|, max(...) and min(...) are all illegal as written. Two tricks fix almost every case, and both have been examined.

Trick 1: minimising an absolute value

To minimise ∑i |gi(x)|, introduce a new variable ei for each term and write

min  e₁ + ... + eₙ
s.t. eᵢ ≥  gᵢ(x)     for all i
     eᵢ ≥ -gᵢ(x)     for all i

Why it works. The two constraints say ei ≥ max(gi, -gi) = |gi|. Since the objective is minimising the sum of the ei, nothing pushes them higher than they must be, so at an optimum each ei is exactly |gi|.

This only works when the objective pushes ei down. Trying it inside a maximisation lets ei float up to infinity and the model is wrong.

Trick 2: minimising a maximum

To minimise maxi |gi(x)|, use a single variable t:

min  t
s.t. t ≥  gᵢ(x)     for all i
     t ≥ -gᵢ(x)     for all i

One variable bounded below by every term is bounded below by their maximum, and minimising drives it down to exactly that maximum.

One ei each gives the sum of absolute errors. One shared t gives the maximum absolute error. That single difference is the whole distinction, and it has been examined both ways.

Line fitting, the recurring example

Given points (xi, yi), fit y = ax + b. Least squares minimises ∑(axi + b - yi)2, which is not an LP, though it does have a closed form. Minimising ∑|axi + b - yi| is an LP by trick 1 and is less sensitive to outliers. Minimising the worst single error is an LP by trick 2. Variables are a and b, both free, so both get split when you go to standard form.

What the LP graders actually mark you down for

Say what your variables mean, in English, before you write a single constraint. Students who skip this are the ones who then get confused about what they are solving. One line is enough: "let xi,j be the kilograms per month sent from roaster i to shop j".

Know which symbols are variables and which are constants. Things like cj, dj and wi,j are given numbers. They must never appear in the non-negativity constraints. Writing wi,j ≥ 0 signals you do not know what you are optimising over.

The dual is not the negative of the primal. And no primal variable may appear anywhere in the dual: if an x survives into your dual, the result is not even linear.

Count before you write. If the primal has m + n constraints, the dual has m + n variables, not m × n. Getting this count wrong is the single most common way to lose the second half of the question.

Derive the dual constraint by comparing coefficients, one primal variable at a time. Do not sum over all the inequalities at once. For each xi,j, ask what its coefficient is on each side; since xi,j ≥ 0, it is enough that yi + zj ≥ wi,j, and that is the dual constraint.

Exam answer, written out

December 2024 Q6, both parts.

The question 
Maximize  5x₁ + x₂
s.t.      x₂ ≤ 1 - x₁
          2x₂ ≥ x₁
          x₁ ∈ ℝ,  x₂ ≥ 0

(a) Convert to standard form. (b) Write the dual, saying which dual variable goes with which primal constraint.

Try it, then open this

(a) Standard form.

Move variables left: x₁ + x₂ ≤ 1 and -x₁ + 2x₂ ≥ 0.

Flip the second: x₁ - 2x₂ ≤ 0.

x₁ is free, so replace it with x₁⁺ - x₁⁻ where both are non negative, everywhere including the objective:

max  5x₁⁺ - 5x₁⁻ + x₂
s.t. x₁⁺ - x₁⁻ +  x₂ ≤ 1
     x₁⁺ - x₁⁻ - 2x₂ ≤ 0
     x₁⁺, x₁⁻, x₂ ≥ 0

(b) The dual. Two primal constraints, so two dual variables. Three primal variables, so three dual constraints.

  • y₁ corresponds to the first primal constraint, x₁⁺ - x₁⁻ + x₂ ≤ 1;
  • y₂ corresponds to the second, x₁⁺ - x₁⁻ - 2x₂ ≤ 0.

The objective coefficients come from the primal right hand side (1, 0). Each dual constraint reads the column of one primal variable:

min  y₁
s.t.  y₁ +  y₂ ≥  5        (column of x₁⁺)
     -y₁ -  y₂ ≥ -5        (column of x₁⁻)
      y₁ - 2y₂ ≥  1        (column of x₂)
      y₁, y₂ ≥ 0
The thing worth noticing

The first two dual constraints are y₁ + y₂ ≥ 5 and y₁ + y₂ ≤ 5, which together force y₁ + y₂ = 5. That is not a coincidence: a free primal variable always produces an equality dual constraint, because splitting it into a plus and a minus part generates a matched pair of opposite inequalities.

The mirror image also holds: a primal equality constraint gives a free dual variable. If you take the dual of a program that was not in standard form and get something with equalities or free variables in it, that is why. Writing the pair of inequalities is safer on an exam, but knowing they collapse lets you sanity check the answer.

The ten second check

Count. Two primal constraints and two dual variables. Three primal variables and three dual constraints. Max became min. Every ≤ became ≥. All variables still non negative. If any of those five does not hold, you transposed something wrong.

Where the marks actually go on Q6
  • Forgetting to substitute the free variable in the objective. Splitting only in the constraints is the single most common error.
  • Skipping "which dual variable corresponds to which constraint". The question asks for it in words, so it is marked, and it is one line.
  • Flipping the objective sense but not the inequalities. Max pairs with ≤ and min with ≥. Mixing them gives a program that is not in either standard form.
